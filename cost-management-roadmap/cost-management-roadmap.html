2The language of cost

Price, cost and value

  • One amount, two names. Price is revenue to the seller; cost is what the buyer gives up.
  • Value is neither: worth to a defined party at a defined date. An opinion, not an amount paid.
  • The buyer’s real cost is the installed cost, not the price.

installed cost = 250,000 + 8,000 + 21,250 + 35,000 + 6,000 = 320,250  (1.28× the price)

Was a two-term contrast, with value absent.

Seven cost terms, numbered once

  • 1 Opportunity — the benefit of the best alternative given up. Never in the accounts.
  • 2 Sunk — past outlay that cannot be recovered. Common to every option, so it cannot change the choice.
  • 3 Book — a non-cash cost carried in the accounts; depreciation is the standard case.
  • 4 Cash — an actual outlay. A book cost is recorded now; the cash left earlier.
  • 5 Incremental — the future difference between two options. Costs common to both drop out.
  • 6 Investment (first) — capital to acquire, install and commission, ready for use.
  • 7 Life-cycle — first cost plus operation, maintenance and disposal (ISO 15686-5).

Was the heading and SUNK COST each printed twice.

Reproduction, replacement — and the decision they are not

  • Both are valuation bases, not decisions. They price an asset you own, at today’s costs.
  • Cost approach: value = cost new − depreciation + land.
  • Keep or replace is a separate defender-and-challenger test, on future cash only: enter the asset at market value, never at book value or first cost.

defender $100,055/yr vs challenger $97,063/yr  ·  at book value it reads $150,146, wrong by $50,091 a year

Was captioned as the move-or-buy decision. It is not.

Budget variance

  • Approved budget − forecast final cost = variance at completion. Both signs, always.
  • Positive is headroom. Negative is an overrun: raise a change or draw contingency now.
  • Budget less paid, or less committed, is budget remaining — it ignores the 32.5 still to come.

  • The office rate excludes lab HVAC and exhaust, lab power, racks and casework.

Capacity factored estimating

  • The capacities divide, and n is an exponent. n ≈ 0.6 for plants; range 0.5 to 0.9.
  • +29% capital buys +53% capacity, so -16% per ton per day.
  • Valid 0.5× to 2× the base, same technology. Fixed-route networks use n ≈ 0.2 to 0.3.
  • Escalate and location-adjust first. Screening level only — confirm with quotes.

Staged or ultimate: conditional, not a rule

  • One metric for both branches: NPV of life-cycle cost, one rate, one horizon, one basis.
  • Ultimate wins when escalation plus amortised remobilisation beats the discount rate, and demand is firm.
  • Staged wins when capital is dear, demand is uncertain, or the technology may change.

10-year horizon · 2% real escalation · 15% remobilisation · curves cross at 7.5% real

Was printed as the standing answer, on two different metrics.

The monthly cycle

  1. 1Cut off and accrue — every measure to the same day
  2. 2Update the estimate at completion
  3. 3Bridge the movement from last month’s EAC
  4. 4Issue it, with the trend behind the movement
  5. 5Act on the variance: raise a change, or draw contingency
  6. 6Capture the as-built cost and normalise it
  7. 7Return it to the benchmark set Phase 5 draws on

this cycle is CHECK and ACT in the Phase 1 project-control map

Where each phase sits

  • 1 sets the loop, and says when influence is still worth spending.
  • 2 fixes the words, so two people reading one number mean the same thing.
  • 3 is the loop running: measure, forecast, explain the movement.
  • 4 draws the boundaries, so nothing is counted twice or missed.
  • 5 applies Phase 1’s curve where influence is highest.
